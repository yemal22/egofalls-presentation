Can sound help a camera detect a fall?

What we learned from comparing motion, frame sequences, and audio on unseen recording groups.

Brummel Duasenge · Liantsoa Rantoniaina
Morel Yemalin · Pape Malick
AIMS South Africa · 20 November 2026 · 12 min read

Our main finding: selected audio–video fusion reduced false alarms from 40.57 to 17.76 per 100 non-fall clips, while fall recall dropped from 86.98% to 76.11%. Fewer alarms did not mean a more reliable fall detector at comparable recall.

The question

A sudden movement is not always a fall.

Imagine someone falling while alone, unable to reach a phone. A detector could help trigger an assistance alert. Before that idea becomes useful, it has to answer a difficult perceptual question: did the person fall, or did they simply sit down, bend over, or lie on a bed?

With a wearable camera, the person’s body is often outside the frame. We see the world move around them instead. A tilted room or a fast downward viewpoint change can be evidence of a fall, but it can also be an ordinary activity. Sound might provide another clue: an impact, a vocal reaction, or the pattern of movement around the event.

We asked whether adding audio could reduce false alarms without substantially reducing the number of falls detected. We tested that idea on EGOFALLS, a dataset of staged egocentric activities. We studied recorded-clip classification; alert delivery and clinical benefit were outside our experiment.

Data before models

First, make the comparison fair.

The original EGOFALLS paper reports 10,948 clips from 14 subjects. Our available Kaggle copy was smaller, and we did not assume it represented the complete release. We audited 5,218 files: 245 duplicate-related exclusions and one media failure left 4,972 eligible clips.

Nine further clips were unavailable after video feature extraction. Our final comparison therefore uses 4,963 matched clips, with identical clip identifiers, labels, and fold roles for audio and video.

Matching matters. If audio and video were evaluated on different recordings, an apparent gain could simply reflect an easier test set. The final audit finds no repeated exact file hashes within our cohort. Near-duplicates, paired recordings of the same event, and the mapping from folder names to real participant identities remain open checks.

Before training, we also explored motion and media quality on a sample: 259 of 260 clips completed analysis. Falls tended to show larger motion peaks, but the distributions overlapped. A non-fall could produce a large peak too. That observation motivated a stronger temporal model rather than a rule that equates “large motion” with “fall.”

Video

Two representations of the same event.

Model 1 · Motion + logistic regression

Frame differences and RAFT-Small optical flow describe how strongly the image changes. Eight summary statistics feed a logistic classifier.

Compact and interpretable, but temporal order is largely compressed away.
Model 2 · ResNet-18 + GRU

A frozen ResNet-18 encodes 30 ordered frames. A GRU reads their sequence, and a binary head produces a fall score.

Preserves a sequence representation rather than only whole-clip summaries.

Model 1 measures two signals: absolute frame differences and optical-flow magnitude. For each, we retain the mean, standard deviation, 90th percentile and maximum. We normalise changes by time, and flow displacement by image dimensions. Train-only standardisation precedes logistic regression.

Model 2 follows Liantsoa’s sequence-model approach, adapted to our matched evaluation. Each of 30 full-clip frames becomes a 512-dimensional feature vector. A single-layer GRU with 128 hidden units reads these features; dropout and a linear head produce the final score. ResNet is frozen, while the GRU and head are trained.

The GRU achieved a modest aggregate improvement over motion: 89 fewer false positives and one additional detected fall. Mean recall across the six fall-containing groups rose from 77.7% to 82.3%, and worst-group recall from 39.1% to 65.4%. Some groups still became worse. We selected Model 2 as the video branch for fusion, without claiming it wins for every person.

Video training details

Motion features use a 4 Hz, 160-pixel-wide scan and at most 16 uniformly distributed adjacent frame pairs. Inner leave-one-training-group-out validation selects logistic regularisation C from 0.01, 0.1 and 1 by mean group log loss.

The sequence branch resizes frames to 224 × 224 with ImageNet normalisation. Training uses train-only feature scaling, weighted binary cross-entropy, AdamW at learning rate 0.001 and weight decay 0.01, batch size 32, gradient clipping at 1, up to 50 epochs, and patience 8 on unweighted validation loss. The positive loss weight is the training non-fall/fall count ratio. Seed: 42.

Audio and fusion

Listening supplies a different signal.

The audio team compared three approaches: MFCC features with logistic regression, a log-Mel CNN, and a PANNs-based approach. In their earlier six-group benchmark, logistic regression produced the fewest pooled false alarms, with slightly lower recall. We retained that simple branch for the final matched experiment.

Audio is converted to mono at 8 kHz. We select a five-second window around the strongest positive change in short-time energy, without using the class label. MFCC statistics, their temporal differences, and three amplitude-related features form an 83-dimensional vector. A train-only scaler and balanced logistic classifier return an audio score.

This is a weighted average of probabilities, not raw logits. The video weight α and decision threshold τ are selected on validation recordings, then frozen for testing. We compare audio alone, video alone, equal weighting, and validation-selected weighting.

Two audio studies, two protocols. The earlier audio benchmark used 5,091 clips and six linked groups. The final fusion experiment refits audio logistic regression on our common 4,963 clips and the video model’s seven fold assignments. The results below are from this matched final experiment.

Audio features and fusion selection

The audio frontend uses 20 ms energy blocks, a 25 ms Hann window, 10 ms hop and 256-point FFT. We use 64 Mel filters from 50 to 3,800 Hz and 20 MFCCs. The means and standard deviations of MFCCs and their first differences supply 80 values; log RMS, peak amplitude and zero-crossing rate supply three more. Logistic regression uses C = 1, balanced class weights and L-BFGS with up to 3,000 iterations.

For each fold, α ranges from 0 to 1 in steps of 0.1. For each candidate, we choose the highest observed threshold reaching at least 90% validation fall recall. We minimise validation false alarms; ties prefer least excess recall, weight closest to 0.5, then smaller α. Each baseline also gets its own validation-selected threshold. We do not separately calibrate scores, so α is not an intrinsic measure of modality reliability.

Evaluation

Hold out groups, not random clips.

A random split can place recordings from the same person or recording setup in both training and testing. We instead use seven outer folds. Each holds out one archive identifier for testing and another for validation; the remaining five train the models.

5 groupsTrain models and scalers
1 groupSelect stopping and rules
1 groupTest frozen predictions

Each clip appears once in the pooled outer-test results for each method. Normalisation is fitted on training data only. The GRU’s early-stopping validation group is reused for fusion selection, so it is a shared development group—not a fresh independent validation sample.

We report fall recall, false alarms per 100 non-fall clips, and F1. False alarms per clip are not false alerts per hour. One group, S_JF, contains no falls: its recall is undefined, not zero.

\(\mathrm{Recall}=\frac{TP}{TP+FN},\qquad \mathrm{FA}_{100}=100\frac{FP}{FP+TN}\)

Our proposed success criterion was at least five fewer false alarms per 100 non-falls than video, with recall within five percentage points. Although the fusion notebook specifies this before its evaluation, model families and all seven groups had already been inspected during development. We therefore treat the study as exploratory.

Measured results

Fewer alarms. More missed falls.

All methods in the table use the same final cohort. The video GRU detects the most falls; audio and fusion trigger fewer alarms. Those are different operating points, so judging only one metric would give a misleading answer.

Against video, selected fusion removes 706 false positives while adding 203 missed falls. The 22.80-alarm reduction is substantial, but the 10.87-point recall loss exceeds our five-point tolerance. Our success criterion was not met.

The audio-only control is just as important. Compared with audio alone, selected fusion removes 75 false positives and misses 70 additional falls. Its reduction is only 2.42 alarms per 100 non-falls. Equal fusion also fails to beat audio on both metrics. A fusion gain over video should not be mistaken for a gain over the stronger single-modality operating point.

Adding another modality is useful only if the combined decision improves the outcome we actually care about.

Fusion’s pooled ROC AUC is higher than video’s, which suggests a stronger ranking of the combined test scores. But ranking and thresholded alerts are different questions. Pooled scores also come from separately fitted folds without common calibration. We do not treat these AUCs as proof of a deployable alert rule.

Failure analysis

The average hides the hard cases.

A validation threshold can fail on the next group.

For S_Q, validation on S_R selects α = 0.3 and τ ≈ 0.743, with 90.13% validation recall. On the held-out S_Q recordings, selected fusion detects only 41 of 159 falls: 25.79% recall. Video detects 104. Fusion’s low false-alarm rate therefore comes with 118 missed falls in this group.

This is consistent with a shift in score distributions between groups. We have not established whether camera placement, behaviour, environment, or another factor causes it.

Lying down can look like falling.

The video model flags 226 of 253 Lying clips as falls, a false-alarm rate of 89.33%. Selected fusion reduces this to 111 clips, or 43.87%. Bending improves too: 104 false alarms become 46 among 252 clips. Downward motion and viewpoint changes are plausible confounds, but these activity counts alone do not prove which cues the model uses.

Sound can make a decision worse.

Among 261 Stumbling clips, video produces 72 false alarms; selected fusion produces 95. On S_XL, recall drops from 83.59% for video to 53.56% for selected fusion. A loud unrelated event could attract the audio window selector, but this is a hypothesis for waveform and video review—not a measured cause.

These are measured group- and activity-level failures. The example videos at the top illustrate the dataset. We have not labelled those particular clips as successful predictions or errors.

Interpretation

A useful negative result.

We learned three things. First, motion is informative but ambiguous. Second, a learned sequence representation modestly improved the aggregate video result, while leaving substantial group differences. Third, a simple weighted audio–video score did not preserve recall as it reduced false alarms. The negative fusion result is part of the answer, not something to hide.

Our controls rule out one simple explanation: the final audio branch was not tested on an easier set of clips. Matching data and including both single-modality baselines makes the comparison more credible. However, we have not isolated the value of temporal order itself. A frame-shuffling test or an average-pooled ResNet control would help determine whether the GRU truly benefits from sequence structure.

The human connection is also modest. People can consider viewpoint changes, scene context and sounds together, and our audience guessing game invites that reflection. It was not a controlled human-performance experiment. We cannot claim human-like reasoning or a measured human–model accuracy gap.

What the current experiment cannot establish

These are staged, short clips. Full-clip sampling and energy-peak selection can use evidence after a fall, so this is not a test of causal, real-time detection. Folder names are participant proxies, not independently verified identities. Seven groups, one seed, prior inspection of all groups, and unresolved recording relationships limit generalisation. The available subset does not establish demographic coverage or reliability for older and frail people.

What we would do with another week

  1. Freeze the pipeline and test independently. Use verified new participants or another dataset, with final test labels kept out of development.
  2. Test which cues matter. Shuffle frame order, compare pooled frame features, and introduce audio noise or poor lighting.
  3. Evaluate an actual alert setting. Use continuous recordings to measure false alerts per hour, missed events and detection delay. Separate development groups for calibration and fusion selection would help test transfer.

A future assistance system would also need consent, privacy controls, and an evaluated alert workflow. Our present outputs support research and prototyping; they should not determine emergency assistance.

The question is not only whether a model can recognise a fall. It is whether the rule still works when the next person, camera and room are different.
